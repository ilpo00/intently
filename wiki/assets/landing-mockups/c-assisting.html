Why this direction

Philosophy: the blank box is the enemy. For a shopper who already failed at filters, "type anything" can feel like the same open-ended task that just defeated them. So the guide speaks first and hands them concrete doors to walk through — while keeping the free-text field right there, prominent, never gated behind a wizard. It reframes the whole surface as a consultation from word one, which is the single clearest way to say "this is not a filter."

Best for

The inspiration browser, the gift-giver ("something for my dad who hikes"), and anyone who froze at the empty field. Maximum assistance, lowest activation energy. Reach for this if analytics show people landing and leaving without typing.

Layout

Right-weighted. A quiet brand/how-it-works panel anchors the left; the live conversation occupies the right. Distinct from A (centre) and B (left) — and it teaches the model in three lines before the shopper commits.

Keeps the chips

The situation starters graduate into richer path cards (icon + label + a hint of what's inside) — still chips in spirit, but warmer and more legible as "doors."

On "calm reveal"

Taken most literally here, and it's the right call: the greeting lands, then the paths stagger in, then the free-text field. It feels like a person turning to help you — not a form loading.

What each marker does

  1. The guide greets first. A single warm line that frames the surface as help, not search, and explicitly invites a situation. This is the anti-blank-box move.
  2. Concrete paths, framed as starting points. "You can edit it after picking" — so a tap isn't a commitment, it's a head start. Removes the "what do I even type" freeze.
  3. Free-text stays prominent and open. The pills are an accelerator, never a gate — your own design.md rule ("typing always stays open"). The placeholder shows a hard, multi-constraint sentence filters can't express.
  4. (see left panel) the three-step "how it works" sets expectations: understood → correct it → a curated few.

Where I'd push back. This is the riskiest of the three for your decisive shopper — the one who knew exactly what they wanted can read the greeting + path cards as friction between them and the box. I've mitigated it (free-text is prominent, paths are pre-fills not gates), but if your traffic skews "knows what they want," this over-assists. It's also the most engine-dependent: those path cards promise a guided consultation, so the first turn really must ask a good clarifying question, not just dump a grid. Don't pick C on taste — pick it if the data says people arrive and leave without typing. Otherwise B gives most of the warmth with less friction.
